# Educator Guide — Probability Detective

## Audience and session
Target approximately age 12, with individual differences expected. Suggested focused session: 10–15 minutes plus optional replay.

## Prerequisites
Learners should be comfortable with simple fractions, multiplication, and reading small tables or charts. No prior formal probability course is required.

## Learning objectives
Learners should be able to distinguish theoretical probability from observed experimental proportion; explain why repeated trials can approach a model without being guaranteed to improve on every larger sample; reject the idea that a streak changes the next independent fair coin flip; reason about one-die and two-dice probabilities; and compare game rules using expected points rather than one simulated result.

## Activity walkthrough
### 1. Coin Laboratory
Choose a coin probability, predict an outcome, and run 10, 100, or 1,000 flips. The result table keeps theoretical and experimental values side by side. A streak question gives specific feedback and unlimited retry.

Discussion prompt: “What stayed fixed in the model even when the sample result changed?”

### 2. Dice Investigation
Investigate one fair die or the sum of two fair dice. For two dice, the app shows the 36 ordered routes and makes the six routes to seven explicit.

Discussion prompt: “Why are sums near seven more likely than sums near two or twelve?”

### 3. Fair-Game Designer
Alter scoring rules, inspect exact expected-point arithmetic, simulate 600 rounds, then apply the same reasoning to a fresh two-dice fairness challenge.

Discussion prompt: “Can fair rules still produce an uneven score in one run? Why?”

## Differentiation and co-play
- More support: use preset values, read the table before the chart, and compare 10 vs. 100 trials first.
- More challenge: predict the direction of error before each run, explain why “closer with more trials” is a tendency rather than a guarantee, or invent a new fair game.
- Co-play: an adult can ask the prompts above without taking over controls. The lesson does not rank or shame the learner.

## Persistence and privacy
The app stores only lesson progress in local browser `localStorage`. It collects no name, email, demographics, account data, analytics, uploads, voice, photos, or remote grading data. Reset clears stored progress.

## Model limitations
The simulations are educational models using pseudo-random browser-generated trials. A finite experimental run can differ from theoretical probability. The app does not claim diagnostic value, validated learning gains, or universal age suitability.

## Content references
1. NIST/SEMATECH e-Handbook of Statistical Methods — Probability Distributions: https://www.itl.nist.gov/div898/handbook/ — supports the distinction between probability models/distributions and observed samples.
2. Khan Academy — Probability: https://www.khanacademy.org/math/statistics-probability/probability-library — supports treatment of theoretical/experimental probability, sample spaces, and compound events.

These references informed the mathematical framing; interface text, examples, code, and generated lesson assets in this repository are original.

## Offline follow-up
Use two ordinary six-sided dice and paper. Before rolling, list the 36 ordered pairs and group them by sum. Design a points rule fair in expectation, then roll 30–50 times and compare the observed score with the theoretical model.
