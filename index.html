Probability Detective

Follow the evidence. Keep theory and experiment separate.

Private by design Your progress stays only in this browser. No login, name, analytics, ads, or uploads.

10–15 minute investigation

Can short experiments fool a good detective?

Random results can look strange in small samples. Your job is to make a prediction, run real simulations, and decide what the evidence does—and does not—show.

1

Separate two ideas

Theoretical probability comes from a model. An experimental proportion comes from trials you actually ran.

2

Compare samples

See why 10 trials can wander more than 1,000—without pretending a bigger sample must improve every single time.

3

Check fairness

Use exact probabilities and point rules to decide whether two sides have equal expected advantage.

Prerequisite: comfortable with simple fractions, decimals, multiplication, and reading a table.

Predictions are for thinking, not grading. You can skip or revise any prediction and retry as often as you want.

Investigation 2

Dice investigation

Open case

A fair six-sided die gives each face a theoretical probability of 1/6.

With two dice, sums are not equally likely because some sums can be made in more ordered ways than others.

1. Predict and choose a model

With two fair dice, which sum should appear most often over many rolls? Optional
Hint: count routes

Investigation 3

Fair-game designer

Open case

Expected points = probability of scoring × points earned when that outcome occurs.

A game is fair here when Side A and Side B have the same theoretical expected points per round. Short simulations can still end with different totals.

1. Publish your rules

Roll one fair die each round. Side A scores when the roll is at or below its cutoff; otherwise Side B scores.

2. Predict, then simulate

Before the simulation, what do the exact rules predict? Optional
Hint: do the multiplication

Find each side's chance to score, then multiply by its reward. Compare those two expected-point values—not just the number of winning faces.

Simulation evidence

Design the rules, make a prediction if you want, and simulate.

Completion summary

What the evidence supports

Theory vs. experiment

A theoretical probability comes from the model. An experimental proportion comes from a particular run and can differ from the model.

Sample size

Larger samples often settle nearer to expected proportions, but randomness can still make one larger run farther away than an earlier smaller run.

Fairness

Judge the rules using expected points: probability × reward. A fair model does not guarantee tied points in every simulation.
